Flowers of the Hour
Mentzelia involucrata and Mohavea confertiflora

Beauty and deception

Two plants bloom in a desert wash.

One adorns itself in large flowers, creamy white with touches of salmon and orange. Sand blazing star invests heavily in showy blooms and abundant pollen, making itself not only stunning for humans but indispensable to the specialist pollinators with whom it has developed a symbiosis. Female sweat bees in the genus Xeralictus rely almost exclusively on Mentzelia pollen for their food and the provisioning of their offspring. According to one source, these bees will hook onto the style of these flowers with the back of their abdomen and spin around gathering pollen with specialized pollen brushes on their heads and forelimbs, an adaptive merry-go-round of efficiency. What's more, male sweat bees will lie in wait within these blooms while awaiting potential mates, further fortifying the intimate relationship between needful plant and obliging pollinator.


The latter plant is of a more devious nature. It, too, bears cream-colored petals and numerous dark specks that, squinting, might be said to resemble the stamen-studded interior of a Mentzelia bloom, but the affinity ends about there. The ghost flower is stunningly beautiful in a much different way. In stark contrast to the generosity of the blazing star, the ghost flower earns its continued existence through deceit. Its maroon speckles have a sort of hypnotic effect leading the eye to the flower's center, and humans aren't the only ones entranced by its spell. If one comes at the right time of day, the yellow and red knob that protrudes from the flower's center may be topped by a desperate male Xeralictus bee seeking to mate. This central structure has evolved to resemble the hindquarters of a female Xeralictus burying her face among anthers, and by tricking the males to mate with it the plant itself gets pollinated without offering any sort of pollen or nectar reward. Since pollination can only be achieved when one insect visits multiple flowers, one wonders: do the males know that they have been deceived before they alight to the next dubious blossom?

The story of these two plants and the insects they share is, to me, a microcosm of the endless wonders of ecology. I first learned to love these plants on simpler terms, for their beauty and charm alone, but digging deeper has revealed endless more corridors of inquiry and thought. Such is the wonder of nature, such is the nature of life. What more remains to be found?

Tyler H.
