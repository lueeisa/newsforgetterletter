Adventures of Hannum
I. Flower of an Hour
-Salvia funerea-
Funeral sage

Climb a rock, said the sage. Step upon an arid ledge and share in all of this. The sandy wash, the sheer cliffs of lime, the relentless sun... and my perpetual bloom.

I come to Death Valley with some regularity for many reasons, but high among them is the opportunity to spend time with funeral sage. Yesterday, after performing a balancing act to reach one, I was rewarded with innumerable violet blossoms. I sat and snapped pictures of flowers emerging from impossibly woolly sepals, enraptured. Beneath me, a pair of travelers strolled up the canyon. On a whim, I asked if they wanted a smell of the best the desert has to offer. They said yes, so I broke a stem and dropped it for the two of them to experience. Strangers no longer, we bonded over the intoxicating aroma of the flower and our love of this strange land and its incredible inhabitants.

II. Stone Chameleons
Stone chameleons are rocky outcrops, formations visible on the surface of the earth. To me, they look like giant, thoughtful, landscape-tending lizards.

In my travels, I have met two stone chameleons.

The first faces the Pacific from a sheer sandstone bluff, listening attentively to the endless waves.

The second rests atop a hill of granite and spends its days watching the sun rise and set over Avi Kwa Ame: Spirit Mountain.

Each encounter with stone chameleons has come during a journey of personal growth, and I have learned to understand these beings as watchful protectors.

They are patient and meditative. Uncaring of the changes wrought by sun, wind, water, and time, they are content to spend eternity with their brothers and sisters as part of a beautiful tapestry. On their flanks grow bountiful wildflowers. Birds perch upon their heads and backs to watch, wait, and listen. They seem to say: be not afraid of what may come; devote thyself to that which you find most beautiful and protect it; be a model for others to do the same. In a time of great upheaval, I find solace in their stolid resistance.

The virtues of stewardship which I ascribe to these beings are the lived experiences of indigenous peoples who continue to steward these lands, and have for millennia.

As I wander, wonder, grow and change, I keep the wisdom of these ancients in my heart and seek their presence around each bend. May we all be a bit more like the stone chameleons.

Tyler H.
Poem from Emily A.
Molting

Last week
I was like a lobster
Crabby

My shell constricted me
I had outgrown it
And I couldn’t move around as well as I used to

One day
I stopped completely
Awkward, in the open
And with a loud popping noise
My soul split down the middle
And I willed myself to feel the rush of cold water
As my soft body came out of that shell
Which had protected me for so long
And I panicked

I found myself too soft
For this large ocean
Where anything is possible
And I had to hide in a dark crevice
And remind myself of warm waters and gentle tides
And let the current of benevolence to coax me out
And trust the invisible eye of creation
And find that I had grown

My outer layer had re-adjusted
And I could comfortably appear as myself again

— Emily A.

A Touch of Whimsy for Your Week
from Portland, OR

The world can be heavy, chaotic, complicated and yet painstakingly boring.

At times, life feels so serious and it can be difficult to remind ourselves to appreciate the simple beauty that surrounds us.

I hope these little practices add a touch of silly whimsy to your inner landscape.


When you go outside, imagine the sun (or rain!) is kissing you with every ray (or droplet!).

Give yourself a big kiss in the mirror.

Write an anonymous love note or paint a rock with a heart and leave it for a stranger to find.

- Hannah D.
